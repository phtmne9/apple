## A Scientific Discovery

Back in the 1920s–30s, Soviet botanist and geneticist Nikolai Vavilov, while studying the wild flora of Central Asia, took note of the wild apple trees of the Tian Shan and Trans-Ili Alatau: some bore fruit comparable in quality to cultivated varieties. Vavilov was the first to propose that this region was the centre of origin of the cultivated apple.

Almost a century later, the hypothesis was confirmed at the genetic level: in 2010, an international team of scientists sequenced the genome of the cultivated apple (*Malus × domestica*) and published the results in the journal *Nature Genetics*. The analysis confirmed that Sievers' apple is the main wild ancestor of all modern varieties.

Long before that confirmation, Kazakh scientist Aimak Dzhangaliev played a key role in studying and protecting the species.

The name Aimak Dzhangaliev is inseparable from Sievers' apple. Trained as an agronomist, he served at the front in the Second World War — first commanding an artillery platoon, then a battery — and after the war devoted the rest of his life to science.

Dzhangaliev became the first director of the Institute of Fruit Growing and Viticulture of the Academy of Sciences of the Kazakh SSR. But the central work of his life was years of expeditions into the wild fruit forests of the Trans-Ili Alatau: he studied natural groves of Sievers' apple, collected and described rare forms, and bred and patented 27 clone varieties of wild apple and 16 clone varieties of common apricot from them — 43 invention patents in total.

As early as the 1960s, Dzhangaliev identified a breeding plot of about 200 hectares in the Turgen gorge, at elevations from 1,150 to 1,540 metres above sea level — the "Kuznetsov gorge" (coordinates: N43°21'37.80" E77°38'52.74") — home to the richest natural diversity of Sievers' apple in the Trans-Ili Alatau. In 1990, together with R. M. Turekhanova and T. N. Salova, he selected 20 economically valuable apple forms on this plot, five of which were later protected by inventor's certificates of the Republic of Kazakhstan.

## Proof at the genetic level

It was Dzhangaliev who proved, at the genetic level, what botanists had suspected for decades: Sievers' apple is the direct wild ancestor of modern cultivated apple varieties. His work provided the scientific basis for listing the species in the Red Book of Kazakhstan.

For his contribution to studying and conserving Sievers' apple, Dzhangaliev is known as the "father of apples" — a documentary film was made about his work, and a memorial alley was created in his honour in Almaty.

## A museum in his honour

On June 19, the same day the Sievers' apple educational nature trail opened in the Kuznetsov gorge, a museum dedicated to Aimak Dzhangaliev opened at the same site — devoted to his research and his contribution to the study of Kazakhstan's wild apple.
